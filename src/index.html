              Getting around

              Press M for the atlas: every place you've discovered, as a tree on the left and a map on the right. Click a building or quarter on the map to look inside it, and Travel to walk there directly. Locked places say which pass they want.

              Trees and the axe

              Flowers, weeds and mushrooms are ground cover — walk straight over them. Trees, bushes and rocks are not. Deliver your first errand and an axe shows up as a reward; after that, face any tree and swing to clear it (and leave a stump behind). Ten trees later the jackhammer arrives, which breaks rocks into rubble you can walk over.

              Sound

              Every place has its own score, written on the spot from its tags and tone: the scale turns minor where a folder reads grimly, the timbre follows the top tag, the chords come from the folder's own path. Jukeboxes, records and speakers are audio files — open one and press Play it in this building to hear the real file (in the sample world there's nothing to decode, so it improvises on the name instead). It fades as you walk away and stops when you leave the building. The ♪ button mutes everything.

              Locked quarters

              Some districts are walled and some doors have a keycard reader and a guard. Each map has one pass; run an errand for anyone living on that map and they'll hand it over. Walk up to the reader holding the right card and the guard steps aside for good.

              Opening things

              A file's card leads with a plain description written from its type, its tags and its tone — no analysis on show unless you want it, which is what How the town read it folds away. Contents stay shut until you ask: Read it opens a book you turn page by page (real PDF pages included), Play it here puts audio on in the building, Look at it frames a picture, Watch it plays video. Rooms with a lot of paperwork keep it in bookcases instead of desks — open one and you get the shelf itself, one spine per file, click a spine to read it.

              Backpack and kit

              Pick it up (or E with the card open) drops a file into your backpack, which holds eight. B opens it to look things over, deliver them or drop them. Tools live in there too: deliver your first errand and an axe turns up for trees; fell ten trees and a jackhammer arrives for rocks. Both are achievement rewards, not purchases.

              People

              Everyone you've spoken to goes in the phonebook (P) with where they live and what stands between you, and from then on their name floats over their head in the world.

              Errands

              Residents with a ! over their head will ask you to fetch something they've misplaced: a named file, any object of one kind, or something that reads cheerfully or grimly. They send you to parts of the tree you haven't walked yet. Open a file and press Pick it up to carry it, then take it back to whoever asked — they're marked ? while they wait, ✓ once you're carrying the right thing. The log (J) holds your errands. For more hints, ask people standing near where the thing is — locals know their own streets, and the closer they live to it the more precisely they'll tell you. Delivering earns you a true fact about that folder — its oldest file, its heaviest one, the sunniest thing in it.

              Names

              Folder names get tidied for the signboards — separators become spaces, camelCase splits, common short forms expand (src → Source, ml → ML) — and each place gets a word for what it is: quarters, and buildings named for what's inside them (Gallery, Works, Bank, Library, Depot). The survey card in the corner always shows the real folder name underneath, and file names are never altered.

              How folders become places

              Subfolders make a place big. Twelve or more and it's a region; six or more, a town. Both are maps you walk around, reached by a road gate. A folder with three to five subfolders is a district: a cluster of buildings around a plaza inside its parent's map. Anything smaller is a building. Inside, each subfolder is a room off the corridor; big buildings stack their rooms over several floors joined by stairs at the ends of the corridor, and a large pile of loose files is sorted into rooms by what the files are for — pictures and showpieces get long hallways rather than square rooms. Folders with nothing in them get no building at all. A town's loose files live in its hall. Hidden files and dotfiles are left out.

              Theme and tone

              Words in folder names, file names and sampled file contents are looked up in a fixed dictionary of 17 tags (technology, finance, nature…). The top tag picks the ground, buildings, props and floors. A sentiment lexicon with negation handling scores the text; gloomy places get rain and dead trees, cheerful ones get flowers and warm light.
